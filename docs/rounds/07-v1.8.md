## 15. Seventh Exploration Round (v1.8): Polar Reduction and the Subidentity Theorem

### 15.1 The Polar Reduction Theorem

**Theorem (Polar Reduction).** Let F be a monotone bifunction on a complete lattice L, with derived maps `a(x) = νY.F(x,Y)`, `b(y) = μX.F(X,y)`. If a and b have two common fixed points z ⊑ z′, then:

1. **Interval stability:** F maps [z, z′]² into [z, z′].
2. **Derived-map restriction:** the restriction G = F|_{[z,z′]²} is a monotone bifunction on the interval lattice [z, z′], and its derived maps are exactly a and b restricted to the interval.
3. **Polarity:** on the interval, z is the bottom and z′ the top, both common fixed — a *polar configuration*: `m′ = ⊥ = z`, `n′ = ⊤ = z′`, `m′ ≠ n′`.

*Proof.* z, z′ common fixed gives `F(z,z) = z` and `F(z′,z′) = z′`: fixed points of a satisfy `a(w) = F(w, a(w))`, so `z = a(z) = F(z, a(z)) = F(z,z)`; dually for z′. Stability: for x, y ∈ [z,z′], monotonicity in each argument gives `z = F(z,z) ⊑ F(z,y) ⊑ F(x,y) ⊑ F(x,z′) ⊑ F(z′,z′) = z′`. Derived maps: a is monotone with `a(z) = z`, `a(z′) = z′`, so it maps the interval into itself; since a(x) is the *greatest* fixed point of `y ↦ F(x,y)` globally and lies in the interval, it remains greatest within the interval — dually for b. Polarity: z is a-fixed and is the bottom of the interval, so `m′ = μ(a|) = z`; dually `n′ = ν(b|) = z′`. If a and b commute, so do their restrictions (both preserve the interval). ∎

**Corollary (polar form of the Commutation Lemma).** The Commutation Lemma holds **iff there is no commuting polar configuration** — no monotone bifunction on any complete lattice whose derived maps commute and have both `⊥ ≠ ⊤` as common fixed points.

*Proof.* (⟸) Contrapositive: a commuting pair with two common fixed points z ⊏ z′ restricts, by the Theorem, to a commuting polar configuration on [z, z′]. (⟹) A polar configuration is itself a counterexample. ∎

The entire remaining gap for the Commutation Lemma is now a *boundary* statement about a single lattice with a single bifunction: rule out polar configurations.

**Boundary repulsion (necessary conditions for a polar configuration).** In a polar configuration, for every `y ≻ ⊥`: `F(⊥, y) ⊏ y`, and for every `x ≺ ⊤`: `F(x, ⊤) ≻ x`.

*Proof.* If `F(⊥, y) ⊒ y` for some `y ≻ ⊥`, then y is pre-fixed for `v_⊥ : y ↦ F(⊥, y)`, so the greatest fixed point satisfies `a(⊥) ⊒ y ≻ ⊥ = a(⊥)` — contradiction. Dually at the top. ∎

The bottom row of a polar F must sit strictly below the diagonal except at ⊥, and the top column strictly above the diagonal except at ⊤ — the boundary "repels".

**Computational confirmation.** Every equality violator (`m ≠ n`) in the chain censuses passes polar reduction exactly, and satisfies boundary repulsion:

| Lattice | Monotone bifunctions | m ≠ n violators | Polar reduction | Boundary repulsion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chain 3 | 175 | 20 | 20/20 | 20/20 |
| Chain 4 | 24,696 | 7,280 | **7,280/7,280** | **7,280/7,280** |

In every violator, the common-fixed set has `z ⊏ z′`, the interval is F-stable, the restricted derived maps match the originals, and `m′` / `n′` land on the interval bottom / top. The chain-4 census adds 12,450 commuting cases with zero violations, bringing the cumulative Commutation Lemma validation to **57,545 commuting cases across four lattice families (chain 3, chain 4, square B2, diamond M3), zero failures**.

### 15.2 The Extended Dialgebraic Lambek Theorem

**Theorem.** Let F, G be endomaps (endofunctors) with pointwise **subidentity** `F ⊑ Id`, `G ⊑ Id` and the **exchange law** `F∘G ⊑ G∘F`. Then the initial (F, G)-dialgebra has `δ₀` an isomorphism.

*Proof.* Let A₀ be the least X with `F(X) ⊑ G(X)`. By subidentity, `X′ := F(A₀) ⊑ A₀`. Exchange and monotonicity make X′ a dialgebra: `F(X′) = F(F(A₀)) ⊑ F(G(A₀)) ⊑ G(F(A₀)) = G(X′)`. Since `X′ ⊑ A₀` and A₀ is least, `F(A₀) = A₀`. Now `X″ := G(A₀) ⊑ A₀` is also a dialgebra: `F(G(A₀)) ⊑ G(F(A₀)) = G(A₀) ⊑ G(G(A₀))`, using `F(A₀) = A₀ ⊑ G(A₀)` and monotonicity of G. Leastness forces `G(A₀) = A₀`. Hence `F(A₀) = G(A₀)` and δ₀ is the identity — an isomorphism. ∎

**Poset vacuity — and why the theorem still matters.** On a poset category the subidentity class is degenerate: `F(⊥) = ⊥ = G(⊥)` forces A₀ = ⊥ with δ₀ trivially iso. The full censuses confirm 100% iso: chain 4: **196/196** (141 also with exchange); M3: **225/225** (198 also with exchange). The theorem's content lies in categories where subidentity endofunctors are not bottom-absorbing (e.g. Set-theoretic natural subobject functors `F(X) ⊆ X`, `G(X) ⊆ X`): there "leastness" is exactly initiality, and the exchange law is precisely what makes `F(A₀)` itself carry a dialgebra structure. This is the second clean positive class of the dialgebraic layer, alongside the (F, Id) Lambek slice — but, like that slice, it degenerates in poset models.

### 15.3 Failure-zone mining, second wave: exchange laws (negative) — and a census refinement

Conditioning the censuses on exchange-law invariants still fails to separate the iso zone from the failure zone (iso rates among pairs admitting an initial dialgebra, strict existence criterion):

| Condition | Chain 4 | M3 |
|---|---|---|
| Baseline | 62.5% | 83.6% |
| `F∘G ⊑ G∘F` (exchange) | 60.7% | 84.1% |
| `G∘F ⊑ F∘G` (dual exchange) | 73.1% | 83.1% |
| Both (⟹ `F∘G = G∘F`) | 77.5% | 85.2% |
| `F ⊑ Id` ∧ exchange ∧ ¬(`G ⊑ Id`) | 19.0% | 78.8% |
| `G ⊒ Id` ∧ exchange | 48.5% | 64.8% |
| `F ⊑ Id` ∧ `G ⊑ Id` | **100%** (196/196) | **100%** (225/225) |

Both exchange directions together give only a mild lift; one-sided exchange with the other functor non-subidentity actively *hurts* (19.0% on chain 4). Together with §13.3 (preservation invariants: negative), the conclusion hardens: **no nontrivial checkable invariant — preservation type or commutation type — predicts δ₀ iso-ness beyond the two degenerating positive classes** ((F, Id) Lambek slice; doubly-subidentity with exchange). The failure zone is genuinely structural.

**Census refinement (corrects §12.2's M3 figures).** The v1.5 M3 census counted a dialgebra as existing whenever `{X : F(X) ⊑ G(X)} ≠ ∅` (31,062 pairs, 82.8% iso). Strictly, an initial dialgebra exists only when that set has a *least* element — which fails when it has two incomparable minimal objects. Under the strict criterion: **29,999** M3 pairs admit an initial dialgebra, **25,073** of which have `δ₀` iso (**83.6%**). Initial and final dialgebra *counts* still coincide exactly (29,999 / 29,999, with identical iso counts 25,073 / 25,073 — the aggregate duality symmetry of D5 survives), but per-pair existence coincides for 28,996 pairs only: 1,688 pairs have exactly one of the initial/final dialgebra. The qualitative conclusions of §12 (substantial failure zone, branching helps, D5 symmetry in the aggregate) are unchanged. Chains are unaffected (nonempty ⟺ least exists there).

### 15.4 Remaining open problems (after round seven)

1. **The polar gap:** prove that no commuting polar configuration exists — no monotone bifunction on a complete lattice whose derived maps commute and have both `⊥ ≠ ⊤` as common fixed points. This is the sharp final form of the Commutation Lemma gap (§14.3). Boundary repulsion (§15.1) supplies the necessary edge conditions: the bottom row strictly below the diagonal except at ⊥, the top column strictly above except at ⊤. Cumulative evidence: 57,545 commuting cases, zero failures. *Resolved negatively in general and sharpened (v1.9, §16): commuting polar configurations exist on infinite lattices ([0,1] counterexample), so the lemma is a finiteness phenomenon; the correct conjecture is the finite version, proven for chains ≤ 6 and all lattices of height ≤ 3.*
2. **Failure zone (structural):** characterize the δ₀ non-iso pairs structurally. Preservation invariants (§13.3) and exchange invariants (§15.3) both fail as predictors; the two 100% classes degenerate in poset models. M3's higher strict iso rate (83.6% vs 62.5% on chain 4) still indicates that branching of the ambient category is the decisive structural factor.
